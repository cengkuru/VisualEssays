Transparency rose.
Delivery didn't.

Independent assurance means outside reviewers checked whether public infrastructure projects were published, monitored, and delivered properly. We read 95 such reports from 2011 to 2021. The year-by-year story below uses the 49 reports from 2015 to 2021, where the data is dense enough to compare: the transparency standard climbing into law, and the delivery failures it was meant to expose still refusing to move.

The paperwork got better fast

30% → 100%

Year by year, more reports record the disclosure standard written into national law: published budgets, contracts, and progress, checked by outsiders. By 2020 every report in the set describes it as routine. This is the real, hard-won win.

It never comes down

88% to 100%

Every single year from 2015 to 2021, nine in ten reports or more flag the same delivery failures. The line meant to fall as transparency rose stays pinned to the ceiling.

70 of 95 reports flag overruns · 57 flag weak preparation

The gap is the finding

Transparency became law. The delivery failures it was meant to expose did not move. A decade of disclosure made the problems visible. It did not, on this record, make them go away.

What the reports keep finding, across the whole decade

The same failures, report after report

These are not one bad country or one bad year. Counted across all 95 reports, the recurring findings are remarkably consistent.

What the reports say would close it

Disclosure was the entry point, not the destination. The reports converge on what comes next: get project preparation right before tender (57 reports flag it), keep supervision genuinely independent of the contractor (23 reports flag the conflict), and publish the audit and evaluation reports that 23 reports found missing. Transparency opened the books. Delivery is the chapter still unwritten.

What changed, what didn't

A decade of assurance reports, scored on the questions that matter. The wins are real. So is the work left.

The decision for the next decade

The next decade is not about publishing more PDFs. It is about making published evidence trigger something: better preparation before tender, supervision that is independent of the contractor, audits that actually appear, and consequences when a project fails. Transparency proved it can open the books. The open question is whether anyone acts on what they show.

Method and limits

Each report was read with a language model against a fixed schema; every field is as stated in the document, null where the report does not state it, never inferred. Of 95 reports, 55 state a year and 27 state an overall disclosure rate; the year lines use 2015 to 2021, the window with enough reports per year to be meaningful (49 reports). "Delivery failure" means the report flags at least one of: cost or time overrun, weak project preparation, or compromised supervision. These are findings the reports make, not independently audited outcomes; they show what the assurance process surfaced, not a verified national delivery statistic. Counts are directional across differing country methodologies and years.
