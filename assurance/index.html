Transparency rose.
Delivery didn't.

Over a decade, governments across a dozen countries got dramatically better at one thing: publishing what their public projects cost and how they were run. The rules became law almost everywhere. And yet, across the very same projects, the failures that openness was meant to drive out, the overruns, the weak planning, the contractor left to watch itself, barely moved. Every overrun is a school built late, a clinic over budget, a road that still chokes the homes beside it.

We read 95 independent assurance reports, 2011 to 2021. The year-by-year story uses the 49 from 2015 to 2021, where the data is dense enough to compare.

One finding, a dozen countries

The reports come from twelve countries on four continents. Travel to each one and the same thing has happened: the rules to make infrastructure honest became law, and the failures those rules were meant to end stayed almost exactly where they were. The same machine, breaking in the same places

The fixes are not exotic

Overruns are the symptom everyone sees. But the reports trace them to the same machine, breaking at three points: before tender, during construction, and after completion. With no audit at the end, the next project repeats the last one's mistakes.

70 of 95 reports still flagged a project late or over budget. Here is why.

None of the fixes is exotic: finish preparation before tender, keep supervision independent of the contractor, and publish the audit so the next project can learn. All of it sits downstream of disclosure, where the standard has not yet reached.

One win. Four warnings.

A decade of assurance, scored. Transparency entered the rulebook. The delivery controls that should turn published evidence into better projects did not keep pace. All counts are out of 95 reports.

Transparency opened the books.

Delivery is the chapter still unwritten.

The next decade is not about publishing more PDFs. It is about making the evidence already on the page trigger something: better preparation, independent supervision, audits that appear, consequences when a project fails. The standard proved it can open the books. Whether anyone acts on what they show is the only question left.

Method, limits, and the data

Each report was read with a language model against a fixed schema; every field is as stated in the document, null where the report does not state it, never inferred. Of 95 reports, 55 state a year and 27 state an overall disclosure rate; the year lines use 2015 to 2021, the window with enough reports per year to be meaningful (49 reports). "Delivery failure" means the report flags at least one of: cost or time overrun, weak project preparation, or compromised supervision. These are findings the reports make, not independently audited outcomes; they show what the assurance process surfaced, not a verified national delivery statistic. Counts are directional across differing country methodologies and years.
